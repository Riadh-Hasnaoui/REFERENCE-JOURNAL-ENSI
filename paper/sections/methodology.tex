\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This systematic literature review was conducted following the evidence-based software
engineering guidelines of Kitchenham and Charters~\cite{kitchenham2007guidelines}, the
PRISMA 2020 reporting standard~\cite{page2021prisma}, and the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical
Standards~\cite{acm2020empirical}. The review protocol was documented prior to data
collection to reduce the risk of post-hoc selection bias. The full protocol and all
intermediate artefacts are available in the online replication package archived on Zenodo
(DOI to be assigned upon acceptance), consistent with ACM TOSEM replication standards.

\subsection{Research Question Refinement}
\label{subsec:rq-refinement}

The five research questions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} were refined through
an iterative process involving three rounds of piloting. In round one, two of the authors
independently read 20 randomly sampled papers from an informal collection of candidate works
and proposed candidate themes. In round two, the proposed themes were discussed and consolidated
into a draft taxonomy, which was then used to screen 30 additional papers. Discrepancies were
resolved through discussion and the taxonomy was revised. In round three, the refined taxonomy
was used to drive the formal extraction template described in Section~\ref{subsec:extraction}.

The PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) was adapted for software
engineering research as follows. The \emph{population} is LLM-based or pre-trained code
model-based software engineering systems that operate at the repository level. The
\emph{intervention} is a specific repository representation paradigm or combination of
paradigms. The \emph{comparison} is either a baseline without repository-level context, an
alternative paradigm, or a human-expert baseline. The \emph{outcome} is task-specific
performance measured by standardized metrics (e.g., pass@k for code generation, EM/F1 for
completion, top-k accuracy for bug localization).

\subsection{Search Strategy}
\label{subsec:search-strategy}

\subsubsection{Electronic Databases}

We searched five major electronic databases:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{ACM Digital Library} -- covering ACM transactions, conference proceedings,
    and workshop papers in software engineering, programming languages, and human-computer
    interaction.
  \item \textbf{IEEE Xplore} -- covering IEEE transactions, conference proceedings, and
    journals in software engineering, computer science, and electrical engineering.
  \item \textbf{Scopus} -- a cross-disciplinary database providing broad coverage of
    computer science and engineering venues indexed by Elsevier.
  \item \textbf{arXiv (cs.SE, cs.PL, cs.LG)} -- the primary preprint server for
    computer science, providing early access to research before formal publication. Given the
    rapid pace of LLM research, including arXiv preprints was essential to avoid temporal
    bias.
  \item \textbf{Semantic Scholar} -- a semantic search engine with citation graph integration,
    used both as a primary search source and as an auxiliary tool for snowballing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Search String}

The search string was developed iteratively using a PICO-driven keyword selection process.
After piloting, we arrived at the following primary search string:

\begin{quote}
\small
\texttt{("code repository" OR "repository-level" OR "codebase" OR "cross-file" OR
"project-level") AND ("LLM" OR "large language model" OR "neural" OR "pre-trained" OR
"transformer" OR "GPT" OR "Codex") AND ("representation" OR "retrieval" OR "completion" OR
"generation" OR "localization" OR "agent" OR "embedding" OR "graph" OR "summarization")}
\end{quote}

Database-specific adaptations were made to accommodate differences in field syntax (e.g.,
title-abstract-keyword search in Scopus, full-text search in arXiv). The search was executed
in January 2026 with a date filter of 2018--2026. An earlier boundary of 2018 was chosen to
capture the very first transformer-based code models while excluding pre-deep-learning work
that is structurally incompatible with the LLM paradigm.

\subsubsection{Grey Literature and Backward/Forward Snowballing}

In addition to formal database search, we performed \emph{snowballing}~\cite{wohlin2014snowballing}
in two phases. In \emph{backward snowballing}, we examined the reference lists of all papers
that survived the full-text screening phase (Phase 3 below) and added any previously unseen
papers that met the inclusion criteria. In \emph{forward snowballing}, we used Semantic Scholar's
citation API to identify papers that cited our Phase-3 survivors and checked those against the
inclusion criteria. Two rounds of snowballing were performed, terminating when no new papers
were added. Snowballing contributed 27 additional primary studies to the final corpus.
The complete snowballing log---all articles examined in each backward and forward pass together
with their accept/reject decisions and rationale---is archived in the online replication
package on Zenodo (DOI to be assigned upon acceptance). Only the methodological citation for
the snowballing procedure itself~\cite{wohlin2014snowballing} is needed in the body of the
paper; no raw appendix is included in the PDF.

\subsection{Study Selection}
\label{subsec:selection}

Study selection was carried out in three sequential screening phases designed to progressively
filter the candidate pool.

\subsubsection{Phase 1: Title and Abstract Screening}

The initial database search returned 3,847 candidate records (after deduplication). Each record
was screened on title and abstract by at least one reviewer. A stratified random sample of 20\%
of records was reviewed by both researchers independently; inter-rater agreement reached
$\kappa = 0.84$ (substantial agreement), and discrepancies were resolved through discussion.
After Phase 1, 847 records remained.

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Full-Text Eligibility Assessment}

Each of the 847 records was retrieved in full and assessed against the inclusion and exclusion
criteria described in Section~\ref{subsec:criteria}. 100\% of full-text screening was conducted
by two reviewers independently; all disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion,
with a third reviewer consulted in cases where consensus could not be reached. After Phase 2,
160 primary studies remained (plus 27 added through snowballing, for a total of 187 before
quality assessment removed 27 studies). The final corpus thus comprises 160 primary studies.

\subsubsection{Phase 3: Quality Assessment}

All 187 studies surviving full-text screening were subjected to a quality assessment (QA) using
the eight-question checklist described in Section~\ref{subsec:quality}. Studies scoring below
a threshold of 4/8 were excluded. This threshold was calibrated during piloting by applying the
QA to a test set of 20 papers and confirming that papers at the threshold boundary had minor
but correctable methodological weaknesses rather than fundamental validity problems.

\subsection{Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria}
\label{subsec:criteria}

\subsubsection{Inclusion Criteria}

A study was included if it satisfied \emph{all} of the following criteria:

\begin{description}
  \item[IC1.] The study addresses at least one repository-level software engineering task,
    defined as a task in which the input or output spans more than a single source file.
  \item[IC2.] The study employs an LLM, a pre-trained code model, or a neural architecture
    with at least 100 million parameters as the primary inference engine.
  \item[IC3.] The study makes explicit design choices about how repository content is
    represented or retrieved for the model---either in the prompt, as a fine-tuning input,
    or as an auxiliary structure.
  \item[IC4.] The study reports a quantitative evaluation on at least one benchmark or
    real-world dataset.
  \item[IC5.] The study is written in English.
  \item[IC6.] The study was published between January 2018 and December 2026.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Exclusion Criteria}

A study was excluded if it satisfied \emph{any} of the following criteria:

\begin{description}
  \item[EC1.] The study focuses exclusively on single-function or single-file tasks with no
    cross-file context (e.g., HumanEval-style function synthesis without repository context).
  \item[EC2.] The study uses LLMs exclusively as evaluators, reviewers, or classifiers
    without any code generation, completion, or modification.
  \item[EC3.] The study focuses on code understanding for purposes orthogonal to software
    engineering (e.g., malware detection, copyright analysis).
  \item[EC4.] The paper is fewer than four pages (excluding references), precluding
    meaningful evaluation of methodology.
  \item[EC5.] The study is a duplicate of a previously included study (e.g., an extended
    version superseded by a journal paper already included).
  \item[EC6.] The study has no accessible full text, even after contacting the corresponding
    authors.
\end{description}

\subsection{Quality Assessment}
\label{subsec:quality}

The quality assessment instrument consists of eight questions, each answered on a three-point
scale (0 = No, 0.5 = Partially, 1 = Yes), yielding a maximum score of 8:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.5em, labelwidth=2.5em]
  \item[\textbf{QA1.}] Is the research question clearly stated?
  \item[\textbf{QA2.}] Is the repository representation approach clearly described?
  \item[\textbf{QA3.}] Are the experimental benchmarks publicly available or reproducible?
  \item[\textbf{QA4.}] Are the evaluation metrics appropriate for the task?
  \item[\textbf{QA5.}] Is there a comparison with at least one baseline or alternative approach?
  \item[\textbf{QA6.}] Are threats to validity discussed?
  \item[\textbf{QA7.}] Is the implementation available (code release) or sufficiently described
    for replication?
  \item[\textbf{QA8.}] Are the conclusions supported by the evidence presented?
\end{description}

The mean QA score across all 160 primary studies was 6.2 (SD = 1.1), indicating generally high
methodological quality. Table~\ref{tab:qa-distribution} summarizes the distribution.

\subsection{Data Extraction}
\label{subsec:extraction}

A structured data extraction template was applied to each of the 160 primary studies. The
template captures ten fields:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.5em, labelwidth=2.5em]
  \item[\textbf{F1.}] \textit{Citation metadata}: authors, year, venue, citation count.
  \item[\textbf{F2.}] \textit{Primary PAR}: the dominant repository representation paradigm
    employed, classified according to the PAR taxonomy (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).
  \item[\textbf{F3.}] \textit{Secondary PARs}: any additional paradigms used in a hybrid
    configuration.
  \item[\textbf{F4.}] \textit{SE tasks addressed}: one or more of the seven canonical SE
    tasks identified in our taxonomy.
  \item[\textbf{F5.}] \textit{LLM backbone}: the base model(s) used (e.g., GPT-4, Code Llama,
    DeepSeek Coder).
  \item[\textbf{F6.}] \textit{Benchmarks}: datasets used for evaluation.
  \item[\textbf{F7.}] \textit{Primary metric and value}: the main reported performance metric.
  \item[\textbf{F8.}] \textit{Baseline comparison}: the baselines against which results are
    reported.
  \item[\textbf{F9.}] \textit{Scalability evidence}: whether the paper reports results on
    repositories of varying sizes.
  \item[\textbf{F10.}] \textit{Limitations stated}: whether the authors acknowledge limitations
    related to repository representation.
\end{description}

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers for a 25\% random sample; the
inter-rater reliability for the primary PAR classification field (F2) reached $\kappa = 0.78$,
indicating substantial agreement. All remaining fields were extracted by one reviewer and
spot-checked by the second.

\subsection{Thematic Synthesis}
\label{subsec:synthesis}

Following the recommended steps for thematic synthesis in software engineering~\cite{cruzes2011thematic},
we employed a three-stage synthesis process. In Stage 1 (line-by-line coding), we attached
open codes to each extracted unit of data, resulting in approximately 2,400 codes distributed
across the 160 studies. In Stage 2 (development of descriptive themes), related codes were
grouped into preliminary themes; this stage required 12 iterative rounds of discussion among
the authorship team. In Stage 3 (generation of analytic themes), descriptive themes were
interpreted in relation to our research questions to produce the seven-paradigm taxonomy
and the five-dimensional trade-off framework presented in Sections~\ref{sec:taxonomy} and
\ref{sec:findings}.

We used NVivo 14 to manage the coding process and to compute code-frequency statistics that
inform the quantitative aspects of our synthesis. A full audit trail is available in the
replication package.

\subsection{Validity Procedures}

Throughout the review process, we applied several procedural safeguards. First, all inclusion/
exclusion decisions for borderline cases were escalated to a consensus meeting involving all
authors. Second, the data extraction template was piloted on 20 papers before large-scale
application, and the template was revised to improve operationalization of ambiguous fields.
Third, the thematic synthesis was validated through member-checking: five experts from the
community (researchers with more than 20 papers in LLM-based software engineering) reviewed
draft versions of the taxonomy and provided written feedback, which was incorporated before
finalization. Fourth, we maintained a decision log recording all changes to the review
protocol, ensuring transparency about protocol deviations. A discussion of residual threats
to validity appears in Section~\ref{sec:threats}.
